      The brief this page was built from

      “I don’t want a dashboard. Dashboards are where focus goes to die. One screen, readable in five seconds.”
      One column. No charts, no widgets, no tabs. The only graphic is a line showing how much of today is gone.
      “Every morning, ask me the question. Keep my answers. If I keep saying no, tell me. Don’t soften it.”
      The mirror sits at the top. Fourteen days of answers as dots. Three Nos in a row and it says so, in red.
      “Three things. Not five. If I try to add a fourth, make me kill one.”
      The list stops taking input at three. The ten-then-three exercise is there when the three aren’t obvious.
      “Show me what I said no to. I’m as proud of that list as the other one.”
      A running count of declined things, struck through and dated.
      “One ship date. Count it down in days. Not percentages, not burndown charts.”
      One number, very large. It turns red the day after the date passes.
      “No email count. No stock price. No weather. No news. If it’s important, someone will walk into my office.”
      Nothing on this page updates itself except the date and the day line.
      “Make the back of the cabinet as nice as the front.”
      One file, no framework, no build step, no accounts. Your entries stay in this browser.

      The quoted prompts are imagined: what Steve Jobs might ask for today. The ideas behind them are his: the mirror question (Stanford commencement, 2005), “focus is about saying no” (WWDC, 1997), the top-ten list cut to three (described in Walter Isaacson’s Steve Jobs, 2011), and “real artists ship” (Macintosh team, 1983).
